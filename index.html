5.1 Blackbody Radiation

• A blackbody is an idealized physical object that absorbs 100% of all incident electromagnetic radiation regardless of frequency or angle of incidence.

• Classical physics predicted the Rayleigh-Jeans law, which stated that the energy density of radiation inside a cavity scales proportionally to the square of the frequency (\(u(\nu) \propto \nu^2\)).

• This classical prediction resulted in the ultraviolet catastrophe, a catastrophic mathematical failure where the total emitted energy approached infinity as the wavelength approached zero (the ultraviolet spectrum).

• Max Planck resolved this failure in 1900 by introducing the revolutionary postulate that energy exchange between the cavity walls and the radiation field is not continuous, but occurs in discrete packets called quanta.

• The energy (\(E\)) of a single quantum is directly proportional to its frequency (\(\nu \)), connected by Planck's constant (\(h\)).

⚙️ Working Formulas • Planck's Energy Quantization Equation: \(E=h\nu =\frac{hc}{\lambda }\) • Planck's Radiation Law: \(u(\nu ,T)=\frac{8\pi h\nu ^{3}}{c^{3}}\frac{1}{e^{\frac{h\nu }{k_{B}T}}-1}\) • Wien's Displacement Law: \(\lambda _{\max }T=b\quad (\text{where\ }b\approx 2.898\times 10^{-3}\text{\ m}\cdot \text{K})\)

5.2 The Photoelectric Effect

• The photoelectric effect is the physical process where electrons (photoelectrons) are ejected from the surface of a metal substrate when irradiated by incident light of high enough frequency.

• Classical wave theory incorrectly predicted that the kinetic energy of liberated electrons would increase with the intensity (brightness) of the light wave and that low intensities would require a time delay for electrons to absorb enough energy to escape.

• Albert Einstein explained the phenomenon in 1905 by modeling light as a localized stream of particle-like packets of energy, later named photons.

• The experimental data proved that electron emission is instantaneous once the light frequency crosses a specific minimum threshold frequency (\(\nu _{0}\)), and increasing the light intensity only increases the number of ejected electrons, not their individual speeds.

• The minimum energy required to liberate an electron from the metal surface boundary is defined as the work function (\(\Phi _{0}\)) of that specific metal.

⚙️ Working Formulas • \(K_{\max }=h\nu -\Phi _{0}\) • \(\Phi _{0}=h\nu _{0}\) • \(eV_{s}=K_{\max }\implies V_{s}=\frac{h}{e}\nu -\frac{\Phi _{0}}{e}\)
🧮 Derivation: Determining Planck's Constant from Stopping Potential We begin with \(h\nu =\Phi _{0}+K_{\max }\) Substitute \(K_{\max}=eV_s\): \(h\nu =\Phi _{0}+eV_{s}\) Isolate \(V_s\): \(V_{s}=\left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu -\frac{\Phi _{0}}{e}\) This yields \(y=mx+c\), plotting \(V_s\) vs \(\nu\) slope = \(h/e\).

5.3 The Compton Effect

• The Compton effect is the elastic scattering of a high-energy photon (such as an X-ray or gamma-ray) by a stationary, weakly bound target electron, resulting in an increase in the photon's wavelength.

• Arthur Compton observed in 1923 that the scattered radiation contained a shifted wavelength component that depended exclusively on the geometric angle of scattering (\(\theta \)), a behavior that classical electromagnetic wave theory could not explain.

• This phenomenon provides direct, definitive proof of the particle nature of light, demonstrating that photons carry linear momentum (\(p = h/\lambda\)) and obey relativistic conservation laws during microscopic collisions.

⚙️ Working Formulas • \(\Delta \lambda =\lambda ^{\prime }-\lambda =\lambda _{c}(1-\cos \theta )\) • \(\lambda _{c}=\frac{h}{m_{e}c}\approx 2.426\times 10^{-12}\text{\ m}\)
🧮 Derivation: Conservation: \(E_{initial}=h\nu+m_ec^2\), \(E_{final}=h\nu'+\sqrt{p_e^2c^2+m_e^2c^4}\) , momentum vector \(\vec{p}_{photon}=\vec{p}'_{photon}+\vec{p}_e\) → \(p_e^2=p^2+p'^2-2pp'\cos\theta\) with \(p=h\nu/c\) → \((h\nu-h\nu'+m_ec^2)^2=p_e^2c^2+m_e^2c^4\) → simplifies to \(m_ec^2(h\nu-h\nu')=h^2\nu\nu'(1-\cos\theta)\) → \(\lambda'-\lambda=\frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta)\)

Critical Thinking: Why X-ray? \(\lambda_c=0.002426nm\) tiny vs visible 500nm → shift 0.0005% invisible. For X-ray 0.07nm shift 3.5% measurable. Proves photon momentum.
SIMULATION: θ= 90° Δλ=0.002426nm

5.4 De Broglie's Matter Wave

• Louis de Broglie postulated in 1924 that if light waves exhibit particle-like properties, then material particles (like electrons and protons) must also exhibit wave-like characteristics.

• The periodic oscillation associated with a moving material body is defined as a matter wave or a de Broglie wave.

• The wavelength (\(\lambda \)) of a matter wave is inversely proportional to the linear momentum (\(p\)) of the moving mass.

⚙️ Working Formulas • \(\lambda =\frac{h}{p}=\frac{h}{mv}\) • \(\lambda =\frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}}\) • \(\lambda =\frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_{e}eV}}\approx \frac{1.227}{\sqrt{V}}\text{\ nm}\)

5.5 Wave-Particle Duality

• Wave-particle duality is the fundamental principle of quantum mechanics stating that every subatomic entity exhibits both continuous wave-like and localized particle-like properties depending on the experimental apparatus used to observe it.

• Light manifests its wave nature during propagation behaviors like interference, diffraction, and polarization, but reveals its particle nature during localized energy absorption and emission mechanisms like the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering.

• Matter exhibits particle properties through mass localization and collisions, but shows wave behavior when passing through tight crystalline lattices, producing clear diffraction patterns.

• The unified wave-particle link is bridged mathematically by the de Broglie relations, which directly connect structural wave properties (\(\lambda, \nu\)) to physical particle properties (\(p, E\)).

⚙️ Working Formulas • \(p=\frac{h}{\lambda }=\hbar k\) • \(E=h\nu =\hbar \omega \)

5.6 The Electron Double-Slit Experiment

• The electron double-slit experiment provides definitive, direct visual confirmation that material particles like electrons display wave-like interference.

• When a stream of electrons is accelerated through two closely spaced parallel slits, they do not register as two simple columns behind the openings, but instead generate a clear alternating bright and dark fringe interference pattern on a detector screen.

• Even when electrons are fired through the apparatus one at a time, the individual hits build up the exact same wave interference pattern over time, proving that each electron passes through both slits simultaneously as a probability wave.

• Attempting to place a measurement detector at the slits to determine which specific opening the electron passes through immediately collapses the quantum state, destroying the wave behavior and leaving a standard, classical two-column distribution.

⚙️ Working Formulas • \(\beta =\frac{\lambda D}{d}\) • \(d\sin \theta =n\lambda \quad (n=0,1,2,\dots )\)

5.7 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

• The Heisenberg uncertainty principle states that it is physically impossible to simultaneously measure both the exact position (\(x\)) and the exact linear momentum (\(p\)) of a quantum particle with absolute precision.

• This uncertainty is not caused by technical limitations in our measurement equipment, but is an intrinsic mathematical property of all conjugate variable pairs in wave-particle quantum systems.

• Pinning down a particle's exact location requires a highly localized wave packet with a wide spread of component wavelengths, which directly throws off our ability to measure its momentum (\(p = h/\lambda\)).

⚙️ Working Formulas • \(\Delta x\cdot \Delta p_{x}\ge \frac{\hbar }{2}\) • \(\Delta E\cdot \Delta t\ge \frac{\hbar }{2}\quad \left(\text{where\ }\hbar =\frac{h}{2\pi }\right)\)
🧮 Derivation: Single-Slit Diffraction Consequence Let slit width \(\Delta y=d\), diffraction angle \(\sin\theta\approx\lambda/d\), vertical momentum spread \(\Delta p_y\approx p\sin\theta\approx p\lambda/d\) → \(\Delta y\cdot\Delta p_y\approx d\cdot p\lambda/d = p\lambda\) Substitute de Broglie \(\lambda=h/p\) → \(\Delta y\cdot\Delta p_y\approx h\) → agrees with \(\ge\hbar/2\) limit.
