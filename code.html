5.1 Blackbody Radiation

Notes: A blackbody is an idealized physical body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation regardless of frequency or angle of incidence. Classical mechanics failed to explain the blackbody emission spectrum because the Rayleigh-Jeans law predicted that radiated power density approaches infinity as the wavelength approaches zero, an error known as the ultraviolet catastrophe. Max Planck resolved this in 1900 by introducing the hypothesis that energy exchange between radiation and the walls of the cavity happens only in discrete packets called quanta.

Blue=3000K, Red=5800K Sun, Orange=8000K. Peak moves left (Wien) and area increases as T⁴ (Stefan). Dashed classical RJ blows up at short λ.

Working Formulas:
Planck quantization: $E = h\nu$
Energy density: $u(\nu)d\nu = \frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_B T}-1}d\nu$
Wien: $\lambda_{max} \cdot T = b$, $b=2.898\times10^{-3}mK$
Stefan-Boltzmann: $E = \sigma T^4$
Derivation: Mode density $g(\nu)=8\pi\nu^2/c^3$. Classical $\langle E\rangle=kT$ → RJ law divergent. Planck $E_n=nh\nu$ Boltzmann average $\langle E\rangle = h\nu/(e^{h\nu/kT}-1)$ → finite Planck law. Limit $h\nu<>kT$ → exponential cutoff.

5.2 The Photoelectric Effect

The photoelectric effect is the phenomenon where electrons are ejected from a metal surface when light of a sufficiently high frequency shines upon it. Classical wave theory incorrectly predicted that electron kinetic energy should increase with light intensity and that faint light would cause a measurable time delay before emission. Albert Einstein explained this in 1905 by postulating that light travels in localized packets of energy called photons.
DIAGRAM:
Working Formula: $E = \Phi + K_{max}$ where $E=h\nu$ photon energy, $\Phi$ work function, $K_{max}$ max kinetic energy
Complete Derivation of Einstein's Photoelectric Equation:
Let incident photon energy: $E=h\nu$
Let $\Phi$ be work function, $\Phi = h\nu_0$ where $\nu_0$ threshold frequency
Energy conservation for single photon-electron collision: $h\nu = \Phi + \frac{1}{2}mv_{max}^2$
Substitute $\Phi=h\nu_0$: $h\nu = h\nu_0 + K_{max}$
Isolate $K_{max}$: $K_{max}=h\nu - h\nu_0 = h(\nu - \nu_0)$
Equate to stopping potential: $K_{max}=eV_0$
Final: $eV_0 = h\nu - \Phi$

5.3 The Compton Effect

The Compton effect is the inelastic scattering of a high-energy X-ray or gamma-ray photon by a target electron, resulting in an increase in the photon's wavelength. This interaction provides definitive proof of the particle nature of light by showing that photons carry linear momentum.
DIAGRAM - Compton Collision:
Working Formula: $\Delta \lambda = \lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_0 c}(1-\cos\theta)$
Complete Derivation:
Incident photon: $E=h\nu$, $p=h\nu/c$ along x. Electron at rest: $E_e=m_0c^2$, $p_e=0$
Scattered photon: $E'=h\nu'$, $p'=h\nu'/c$ at angle $\theta$. Recoil electron: $E_e'=\sqrt{(p_e')^2c^2+m_0^2c^4}$ at angle $\phi$
Energy: $h\nu+m_0c^2 = h\nu' + \sqrt{(p_e')^2c^2+m_0^2c^4}$ → Isolate radical → Square → $(p_e')^2c^2 = h^2(\nu-\nu')^2+2m_0c^2h(\nu-\nu')$ ---(Eq1)
Momentum x: $h\nu/c = h\nu'/c \cos\theta + p_e'\cos\phi$ → $p_e'c\cos\phi = h\nu - h\nu'\cos\theta$ ---(Eq2)
Momentum y: $0 = h\nu'/c \sin\theta - p_e'\sin\phi$ → $p_e'c\sin\phi = h\nu'\sin\theta$ ---(Eq3)
Square Eq2+Eq3: $(p_e')^2c^2 = h^2\nu^2 -2h^2\nu\nu'\cos\theta + h^2\nu'^2$ ---(Eq4)
Eq1=Eq4 → $-2h^2\nu\nu' +2m_0c^2h(\nu-\nu') = -2h^2\nu\nu'\cos\theta$ → $2m_0c^2h(\nu-\nu')=2h^2\nu\nu'(1-\cos\theta)$
→ $\frac{1}{\nu'}-\frac{1}{\nu}=\frac{h}{m_0c^2}(1-\cos\theta)$ → Using $\lambda=c/\nu$ → $\Delta\lambda = \frac{h}{m_0c}(1-\cos\theta)$
SIMULATION: θ= 90° Δλ=0.002426nm
QUIZ: Shift depends on? Angle Material

5.4 De Broglie's Matter Wave

Louis de Broglie hypothesized in 1924 that if light waves display particle behavior, material particles must correspondingly exhibit wave-like characteristics. A matter wave represents the probability wave behavior associated with any moving mass piece.
DIAGRAM:
Working Formulas: Photon equating $E=mc^2=h\nu=hc/\lambda$ → $\lambda=h/mc$. Generalize $p=mc \to mv$ → $\lambda = h/p = h/mv$
Electron accelerated through V: $K=eV=p^2/2m$ → $p=\sqrt{2meV}$ → $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2meV}$
Derivation: $E=mc^2=h\nu$, $\nu=c/\lambda$ → $mc^2=hc/\lambda$ → $mc=h/\lambda$ → $\lambda=h/mc$. Replace photon momentum $mc$ with general $p=mv$ → $\lambda=h/p$. For electron $eV=p^2/2m$ → $p=\sqrt{2meV}$ → $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2meV}$ → numerically $\lambda(nm)=1.227/\sqrt{V}$
SIMULATION: V= 100V λ=0.1227nm (atomic size)

5.5 Wave-Particle Duality

Wave-particle duality states that all matter and light display both wave properties and particle properties depending on the physical context of the observation. Particle behaviors appear prominently during localized energy exchanges such as emissions, absorption events, and collisions. Wave behaviors appear during propagation events through space, establishing spatial effects like interference and diffraction patterns. Bohr's Complementarity Principle asserts that the wave and particle models complement one another, but both aspects cannot be measured simultaneously in a single experiment.

5.6 The Electron Double-Slit Experiment

DIAGRAM & SIMULATION - Fire single electrons:
Count=0
Blue dots = high probability (interference maxima), orange = low. After many electrons, fringes build even though electrons arrive one by one → self-interference.
The electron double-slit experiment provides definitive evidence for the wave-like propagation properties of matter. When electrons are fired one at a time through a double-slit structure, each electron registers as a single localized impact point on a detector screen. Over time, as millions of individual electron impacts stack up, they build an alternating fringe layout of high and low intensity bands. This result proves that individual particles pass through the setup as a coherent wave split across both channels before collapsing into a specific spot upon detection. The fundamental distribution pattern is governed by Max Born's probability interpretation, where the probability density P(x) of finding a particle at a specific location is given by the squared absolute value of its wave function: $P(x)=|\psi(x)|^2$

5.7 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle and Its Simple Consequences

DIAGRAM - Confinement:
The Heisenberg uncertainty principle states that it is physically impossible to simultaneously measure certain pairs of complementary variable properties with absolute precision. Conjugate pairs include position-momentum (x, p) and energy-time (E, t) combinations. The fundamental position-momentum inequality limits the product of measurement standard deviations: $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \hbar/2$ The conjugate energy-time inequality defines an identical limit for lifetime energy states: $\Delta E \cdot \Delta t \ge \hbar/2$
Working Formulas: $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \hbar/2$ | $\Delta E \cdot \Delta t \ge \hbar/2$ | $\hbar = h/2\pi$
Simple Consequences:
1. Non-Existence of Free Electrons inside Nucleus:
Nucleus diameter $R\approx10^{-14}m$ → $\Delta x\approx10^{-14}m$ → $\Delta p\approx \hbar/2\Delta x = h/4\pi\Delta x = 6.63e-34/(4\pi 10^{-14}) \approx 5.28e-21 kg m/s$ → $p\approx\Delta p$ → $E\approx pc = 5.28e-21 *3e8=1.58e-12J = 9.9MeV$ → For electron to exist inside nucleus needs ~10MeV, but beta decay electrons only few MeV → electrons cannot reside within nucleus.

2. Finite Ground State Energy of Hydrogen Atom:
$\Delta x\approx r$, $p\approx\hbar/r$, $E = p^2/2m - e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r = \hbar^2/2mr^2 - e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r$ → Minimize $dE/dr=0$ → $-\hbar^2/mr^3 + e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2=0$ → $r=a_0=4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2/me^2$ Bohr radius → $E_{min}= -me^4/32\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2\hbar^2 \approx -13.6eV$ → Uncertainty explains why electron does not collapse into proton, shrinking r drives kinetic to infinity.

Reflection - Unit V: Photons and Matter Waves

🧠 Learning Experiences & Content Understanding: Unit V served as my gateway into quantum mechanics, fundamentally challenging how I perceive physical reality. The transition from classical determinism to quantum probability was intellectually demanding but deeply rewarding. I successfully mastered the mechanics of blackbody radiation and Planck's quantum hypothesis, which elegantly solved the classical "ultraviolet catastrophe." Exploring the photoelectric effect and the Compton effect solidified my mathematical and conceptual grasp of light acting as localized packets of energy (photons). The most profound shift in my understanding came from de Broglie's hypothesis and the electron double-slit experiment, which proved that matter possesses wave-like properties. Visualizing an electron traveling through two slits simultaneously as a probability wave helped me truly comprehend the core of wave-particle duality.

🔍 Critical Thinking & Conceptual Insights: Analyzing the breakdown of classical physics forced me to think more critically about scientific progression. Classical wave theory failed to explain why light intensity doesn't affect the kinetic energy of ejected photoelectrons. By analyzing Einstein's photoelectric equation ($h\nu = \Phi + K_{max}$), I realized how a single, elegant paradigm shift could resolve massive experimental anomalies. Furthermore, evaluating the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle allowed me to look past the math and interpret its physical reality. Rather than viewing it as a limitation of our measuring instruments, I now understand that uncertainty ($\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \hbar/2$) is an intrinsic, fundamental property of nature itself. Applying this principle to mathematically prove why free electrons cannot exist inside an atomic nucleus was an eye-opening exercise in deductive logic.

⚠️ Challenges Faced & Lessons Learned: The primary hurdle I encountered was reconciling Bohr's Complementarity Principle with my everyday classical intuition. It was initially difficult to accept that an electron could act as a localized particle upon detection, yet propagate as a spread-out wave during transit. To overcome this conceptual block, I actively sought out online multimedia simulations of the single-electron double-slit experiment. Watching the statistical buildup of the interference pattern fringe-by-fringe bridged the gap between abstract equations and visual reality. The biggest lesson I am taking away from this unit is that physics often requires us to abandon comfortable biases. When looking at the subatomic universe, we must let rigorous mathematical frameworks and empirical data guide our understanding, even if the conclusions defy our everyday experiences.
